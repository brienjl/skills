# Value Wayfinder

Keep a big idea coherent across sessions while finding the smallest useful next investment. Plan around customer outcomes and learning; let evidence change the route.

## Keep the paperwork small

Default to **one compact map and a few actionable tickets** in the user's existing tracker or workspace. Keep the customer hypothesis, biggest risks, roadmap, and resume note together. Aim for a map that can be skimmed in a couple of minutes; link completed detail rather than continually expanding it.

Use the Value Proposition Canvas and Business Model Canvas as thinking tools. Do not require completed canvases, separate assumption registers, scoring systems, or a full product specification. Add a section, field, or document only when it changes a decision or prevents losing important context. Write each fact once.

For a new idea, sketch the map and first tickets. To resume, read the map and relevant ticket. When results arrive, update the decision and next step. Use [planning-artifacts.md](references/planning-artifacts.md) for a compact template or example; read [tickets-and-tracking.md](references/tickets-and-tracking.md) when tracker mechanics are needed.

## Find the value

Start with the ambition, the first customer segment, their situation, and their current alternative, including doing nothing. Ask a few questions that materially affect the next step. Use supplied context and available facts; mark uncertain answers as hypotheses so planning can proceed.

Apply the **Value Proposition Canvas** in plain language:

- **Customer:** What job are they trying to do? What pain matters most? What gain would make a difference?
- **Offer:** What small product or service could help? How would it relieve that pain or create that gain better than the current alternative?

Keep customer needs distinct from the proposed solution. Start with the few needs that matter most. Distinguish user and buyer when it affects adoption or payment; keep different segments separate when their needs conflict. A concise proposition is enough: **For [customer in context], help [outcome] through [offer], improving [observable benefit] over [alternative].**

## Find what could make the idea fail

Check all three lenses, then focus on the biggest unresolved risk:

- **Desirability:** Will these customers choose and use it to get the benefit?
- **Feasibility:** Can we reliably deliver it with the technology, people, data, and capacity available?
- **Viability:** Is the benefit worth the cost, and can funding or revenue sustain delivery and maintenance?

A short risk list is enough. Use the **Business Model Canvas** to spot relevant gaps in channels, funding, costs, activities, resources, or partners; expand only what could change the next investment. Internal and personal projects need sustainable effort and cost, not an invented revenue model.

Choose what to test by the consequence of being wrong and the cheapest credible way to learn. There is no fixed order among the three lenses. Stated enthusiasm, working code, and a sustainable business model are different evidence; do not substitute one for another. Model-generated personas are hypotheses, not customer evidence.

## Make a small, conditional roadmap

Use **Now / Next / Later**, or the user's existing format:

- **Now:** the smallest usable customer outcome or test of the most important uncertainty.
- **Next:** a small next investment, conditional on what Now teaches us.
- **Later:** the broader direction and open possibilities, without speculative implementation detail.

A usable increment should deliver one narrow end-to-end benefit. An interview, mockup, or technical spike may only produce learning; say which it is and what value increment it enables. Include supporting technical work only when it serves that outcome or test.

Before running a test, state **what we believe, how we'll check, what result would change the plan, and how much time or money we'll spend**. Use an observable comparison or threshold where useful; for qualitative work, state what observations would support or challenge the claim. Mark proposed criteria as provisional and be honest about small samples. Add recruitment, measurement, or guardrail detail only as needed to make the test credible.

Stub the few tickets that can be stated clearly now. Leave uncertain future questions as short notes. Each ticket should explain why it matters and what resolves it, with a dependency or evidence condition only when needed.

## Learn, decide, and leave a handoff

Record the result briefly: **what happened, the source/date and important limitations, what it means, and what happens next**. Link substantial evidence from the ticket. Compare against the original criteria; do not rewrite a miss into a success.

Choose or recommend **persevere, pivot, retest, or stop/park**. Distinguish a recommendation from a human investment decision. Completing a test does not mean its claim passed, and finishing a build does not prove customer value. Keep downstream work conditional on the actual result or an explicit decision to accept the risk.

After learning, change the affected roadmap and tickets, retain a short reason for changed direction, and leave the next action plus anything waiting on people or real-world results. Avoid a separate session report. A later session should be able to resume from the map and one relevant ticket.

Planning is ready when the customer value hypothesis, next small step, and decision it enables are clear enough to act on. Missing evidence can remain explicit. A clear plan or empty queue does not establish product-market fit or completion of the ambition.

Plan and research within the user's request. Customer outreach, spending, publishing, and product execution require authorization for those actions; creating a ticket does not grant it.
